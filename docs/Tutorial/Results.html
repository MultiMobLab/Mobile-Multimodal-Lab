    1 Results

    As the primary goal of this paper is to provide a practical manual for building and operating the MMLab, the results of our analyses are outside the present scope. Interested readers are encouraged to visit the Results chapter of our online MMLab Quarto manual for a detailed account of the experimental findings.

    Having described the MI analysis pipeline, we now present the results of this proof-of-concept experiment. As noted throughout, these results are based on a single dyad (the authors) and are not intended for formal statistical inference. To provide a conceptual framework, we consider three hypotheses that a researcher investigating interpersonal synchrony during joint singing might plausibly hold (we stress that these hypotheses are not formally tested here, they serve only as a narrative scaffold): - (H1) Vision facilitates vocal synchrony: dyads synchronize their (joint-singing) vocal amplitude envelopes more strongly when they can see each other than when visual access is blocked. - (H2) Movement further enhances vocal synchrony: vocal synchrony is highest when participants can both see each other and move freely, as co-singing gestures provide an additional coordination channel. - (H3) Vision and movement increase cross-modal physiological coupling: Beyond vocal singing synchrony, the dyad’s physiological signals (ECG, respiration, EMG) become more coupled across participants when both vision and movement are available, reflecting a broader state of interpersonal alignment. To visualize the full extent of interpersonal coupling across all modalities and conditions, we generated a network plot (Figure S10) and distribution plot (Figure S11) for each of the four experimental conditions. Figure S10. Interpersonal Multimodal Synchrony across Experimental Conditions. The four quadrants show the four experimental conditions (Movement X Vision: top-left; Movement X No_Vision: top-right; No_Movement X Vision: bottom-left; No_Movement X No_Vision: bottom-right; ). Each quadrant shows the same mutual information (MI) values in two ways. Left: coupling network. Nodes P1 (blue) are on the left and nodes for P2 (orange) are on the right. Right: cross-modality MI matrix. Columns are P1 signals (blue) and rows are P2 signals (orange). In both plots the signals appear in the same order: audio amplitude envelope, EMG bicep, EMG tricep, ECG, right-index-finger motion in the x, y and z axes, and respiration. Each of the 64 lines in the network, and each of the 64 cells in the matrix, is the MI between one P1 signal and one P2 signal. The value is the maximum MI across lags, averaged over all sliding windows and trials in that condition. Diagonal cells show within-modality coupling, and off-diagonal cells show cross-modality coupling,. The colour of lines and cells, and the thickness of lines, scale with MI strength: dark purple (min 0.0( is low MI and yellow is high MI (max 1.0). One colour bar applies to all panels. The results displayed in Figure S11 point to the following patterns: - Vocal Synchrony (H1 and H2). The within-modality Audio P1–P2 coupling (the diagonal Audio–Audio cell) was highest in the two movement conditions: NoVision × Movement (MI = 0.801) and Vision × Movement (MI = 0.787), compared to Vision × NoMovement (MI = 0.673) and NoVision × NoMovement (MI = 0.587). This pattern suggests that the freedom to move (rather than interpersonal visual access per se) was the primary driver of vocal synchrony in this dyad, only partially supporting H1 (vision facilitates vocal synchrony) but more clearly supporting H2 (movement enhances vocal synchrony). Notably, the NoVision × NoMovement condition yielded the lowest audio coupling of all four conditions, consistent with the idea that restricting both channels simultaneously most strongly impairs vocal coordination. - Physiological Coupling (H3). The heart rate coupling between participants was high across all conditions, with the highest values observed in the NoVision × Movement condition (MI = 1.018) and NoVision × NoMovement condition (MI = 0.866), followed by Vision × NoMovement (MI = 0.805) and Vision × Movement (MI = 0.717). This pattern does not directly support H3 (that vision and movement increase physiological coupling). Rather, it might suggest that cardiac synchrony in this dyad could have been driven by shared respiratory and movement entrainment, rather than by visual contact. Respiration coupling was relatively stable across conditions (ranging from 0.684 to 0.754), suggesting that the shared singing task itself was the dominant factor regardless of visual or movement manipulations. EMG coupling (both biceps and triceps) remained consistently low across all conditions (MI < 0.12). - Cross-Modality Coupling. Beyond the within-modality diagonal, the matrices reveal notable cross-modal patterns. In particular, the heart rate signal from one participant consistently showed elevated coupling with the other participant’s motion signals (e.g., Heart Rate P2 × Motion X P1 = 0.807 in Vision × Movement; 0.858 in NoVision × Movement) and respiration (Heart Rate P2 × Respiration P1 = 0.577 in Vision × Movement; 0.811 in NoVision × Movement). This cross-modal heart rate–motion–respiration cluster likely reflects the physiological interdependence of these systems during physical activity: movement drives respiration, which in turn modulates heart rate. In contrast, the EMG signals showed minimal cross-modal coupling with any other modality, remaining consistently below MI ≈ 0.30 across all conditions and pairings.

    Taken together, these exploratory results illustrate that the MMLab can capture condition-dependent differences in interpersonal multimodal synchrony during a face-to-face joint singing task. The result patterns observed (i.e., stronger vocal synchrony in the Vision x Movement compared to the NoVision x No Movement conditions ) are broadly consistent with what one might expect from two participants engaged in a structured co-singing task, even though not all hypothesized patterns were confirmed (notably, movement appeared to matter more than vision for vocal synchrony, and physiological coupling did not follow the predicted Vision + Movement gradient). We emphasize, again, that these results are purely descriptive. A researcher applying this pipeline to a substantive research question would need to address several methodological considerations before drawing inferential conclusions. For instance, the large number of pairwise comparisons (64 modality pairs × 4 conditions × multiple time windows) substantially inflates the risk of false positives, and appropriate corrections for multiple testing (e.g., Bonferroni, FDR) would be essential. Additionally, it would be important to establish the chance-level coupling through surrogate analyses (e.g., comparing MI values computed on surrogate participants dyads from real-dyad MI values), as some degree of MI between two signals can arise simply from shared stationarity or slow drift, rather than from meaningful moment-to-moment coordination. Finally, the proof-of-concept presented here used a single landmark (right index finger) for the motion modality and a single summary statistic (maximum MI across lags) per window. Future work could explore richer representations of movement (e.g., full-body kinematic features) and alternative coupling measures (e.g., phase-locking value, Granger causality) to capture different facets of interpersonal coordination.
