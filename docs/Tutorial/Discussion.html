    1 Discussion

    The MMLab was developed to provide an accessible, low-cost, and open-source solution for capturing multimodal human behaviors in both lab-based and ecological environments. Rather than a single piece of software or hardware, the MMLab is a suite of freely available resources – comprising the present paper, an extended Quarto book, and an accompanying GitHub repository. Together, they function as an end-to-end blueprint for assembling a multimodal recording system, executing recording procedures, and processing and analyzing the resulting data streams.

    The tutorial-style structure of this manuscript walks the reader through each step of this pipeline. Grounding all procedures in our proof-of-concept experiment (?@sec-exp, a structured interpersonal singing task with a 2×2 (Vision × Movement) design), ?@sec-setup described the setup of the MMLab, covering the choice and configuration of hardware for video, audio, wireless physiology (ECG, EMG, respiration), and the central integration of all streams through the LSL. ?@sec-preproc dealt with multimodal wrangling and stream preprocessing, describing signal extraction from the unitary file, stream segmentation and multimodal re-merging. ?@sec-process covered multimodal signal processing, including full 3D motion-tracking pipeline, extraction of audio amplitude envelopes, heart rate, muscle activation, and respiratory waveforms, ending with the merging of all filtered signals onto a multimodal array. Finally, ?@sec-analysis presents a proof-of-concept analysis using mutual information, quantifying interpersonal coupling across vocal, physiological, and kinematic channels between the two co-singing participants.

    Across these sections, the MMLab was consistently built around five complementary principles. First, in terms of accessibility, all documentation, code, and tutorials are fully open-sourced. The intent is not only to make the tools available, but to make the technical knowledge behind them transparent and reusable. This lowers the barrier to entry for new users and serves as a pedagogical resource for students and technical staff, as well as offering the opportunity for others to contribute and improve on our designs. Second, in terms of affordability, we demonstrated that a lab capable of simultaneously capturing video (for 3D motion tracking), high-fidelity audio, and wireless physiological signals from two interacting participants can be fully assembled for under €10,000 using off-the shelf consumer-grade components and open-source software Third, in terms of portability, the MMLab allows for mobile research: the recording equipment and laptops can be transported inside simple suitcases and reassembled outside of a fixed laboratory room. This opens the door to field-based, ecologically valid recordings that are typically inaccessible to heavy, room-mounted commercial setups. Fourth, in terms of integration and synchronization, the LabStreamingLayer (LSL) serves as the unifying backbone of the MMLab, solving one of the most persistent bottlenecks in multimodal research (i.e., sub-millisecond synchronization of heterogeneous data streams recorded on different machines, operating systems, and sampling rates) without requiring proprietary middleware. Fifth, in terms of modularity, MMLab is intended to beMMLab is intended to be easily extensible beyond the specific configuration described here. Any device with a native LSL outlet (or a simple python wrapper, as we demonstrated for video and audio) can be added to the pipeline, leaving any user (or future developments of MMLab) with opportunities to add or substitute any modality, leaving any user (or future developments of MMLab) with opportunities to add or substitute any modality (e.g., eye-tracking, EEG, additional biosensors, VR headsets, or additional participants) without rebuilding the underlying infrastructure.

    Beyond these technical contributions, the MMLab is motivated by a broader concern about ecological validity in the study of human behavior. Historically, psychological, cognitive, and neuroscientific research has relied heavily on temporally coarse-grained, unimodal measures: self-reports and Likert-scale questionnaires in social psychology (Baumeister, Vohs, and Funder 2007), button presses and accuracy scores in cognitive and neuropsychological work (Maselli et al. 2023; Parsons, Kruijt, and Fox 2019). This was largely a pragmatic choice: until recently, capturing the temporally rich, multimodal reality of behavior was methodologically prohibitive (Bateman 2016; Sanbonmatsu, Cooley, and Butner 2021). However, although these unimodal measures are valuable for many research questions, they are inherently limited for others. For instance, a Likert-scale rating of how often a person gestures during conversation cannot substitute for a quantitative measure of the movement itself –– its speed, amplitude, or coupling with concurrent speech (Pouw, Trujillo, and Dixon 2020). Gestures during conversation are tightly coupled with speech prosody (add classical refs instead of Pouw et al. 2020), modulated by turn-taking dynamics (Kendrick, Holler, and Levinson 2023), and shaped by mutual visibility (Holler et al. 2014; Mol et al. 2011) in temporally extended and distributed processes shaped by their social context (Pouw et al. 2014; Streeck, Goodwin, and LeBaron 2011; Trujillo and Holler 2023). Similarly, emotional and affective states are increasingly understood as dynamic processes that emerge from the continuous interplay between central nervous system activity, peripheral physiology, and overt bodily behavior (D’Mello and Kory 2015; Mauss and Robinson 2009). Capturing such states therefore requires jointly measuring the neural, autonomic, and behavioral dynamics that co-constitute them, rather than any single channel or proxy in isolation.

    In this context, the MMLab is a methodological response to the current and future state of research across these fields. By making the technical infrastructure for synchronized, multi-stream recording available at a fraction of the previous cost, it enables researchers to move from proxies for behavior to direct measurements of behavior and its underlying neural and physiological signals –– and it makes this move available to labs that would otherwise be priced out of such work. Taken together, this matters both for equity of access and for the diversity of research questions that get asked. In this sense, MMLab contributes to a broader movement in which the question is no longer whether behavior can be captured multimodally, but which embodied processes and inter-personal dynamics become tractable objects of scientific inquiry.

    Alongside its strengths, the MMLab comes with some important limitations. The most obvious is the absence of the institutional and commercial support that accompanies off-the-shelf systems. When a lab purchases a commercial motion-tracking system, it gains more than hardware: installation, calibration support, warranty replacements, and a vendor to call when something breaks. The MMLab has none of this. It is a collection of documented components held together by open-source software which shifts the burden of maintenance, troubleshooting, and repair onto the developer community and, ultimately, onto the users themselves. A related limitation is that the MMLab presupposes a baseline of technical literacy. Although we have deliberately documented every step in prose and opted for extensive code comments, the pipeline still assumes some familiarity with Python, the command line, basic networking, and the logic of digital signal processing. The tutorial aims to lower this barrier, but it does not eliminate it.

    The biggest practical challenge remains the robustness of the multimodal setup. Every component of the MMLab is prone to errors – as in any recording setup, commercial or otherwise. While the setup has been validated in a proof-of-concept experiment and the pipeline is written to prevent common issues and support individual troubleshooting, problems will arise, including in configurations we cannot anticipate, since users will adapt the setup to hardware and environments we have not tested. Here we list the most recurring problems. First, synchronization: LSL’s clock-offset estimation can degrade under heavy WLAN traffic, and consumer-grade devices with irregular sampling rates can even violate the assumptions of LSL’s built-in jitter correction (Kothe et al. 2025). In practice, a wired network and manual inspection of inter-stream offsets after each session are essential. Second, data acquisition: Depending on the data stream being recorded, larger setups face bandwidth constraints. For instance, consumer-grade USB cameras drop frames when bandwidth is saturated, so the pipeline has to be built assuming drops will occur. Third, resource contention: pushing too many high-bandwidth streams through a single node can produce gaps in the recorded data via LabStreamingLayer (Kothe et al. 2025), and streams recorded on the same machine compete for CPU, disk, and bus resources, so a problem in one stream can propagate to others. None of these issues are fatal, but all require careful pre-testing and some iterative debugging.

    Finally, the operational limitations above concern whether the system records; a separate question is what it records. The MMLab relies largely on consumer-grade sensors and approximating algorithms. Even a flawlessly executed session will not match the spatial and temporal precision of a commercial marker-based system (consider, for instance, 0.05 mm error for OptiTrack vs. 1.5 cm error for computer-vision motion capture). For many behavioral research questions, however, this ceiling is well above what the analysis requires: the effects of interest in multimodal communication typically live at the scale of gestures, postures, and prosodic contours rather than sub-millimeter displacements, and consumer-grade capture resolves them comfortably. Moreover, the modularity of the MMLab allows precision to be concentrated where a given study needs it: a camera can be positioned for close capture of the face or hands when fine articulatory or manual details is the target, sampling rates and resolutions can be raised for the streams that carry the effect of interest, and less critical modalities can be recorded more coarsely to conserve bandwidth. Rather than delivering uniform high precision at uniform high cost, the MMLab lets researchers allocate fidelity to match their question. The MMLab is thus not a one-click, black-box solution that works automatically in every environment with every device. It is, however, a transparent and extensible one. We intend to continue improving the pipeline, which includes building a community of developers willing to maintain existing functionalities, add new features (e.g. modalities), and document recurring user issues.

    Looking forward, we see the MMLab less as a finished product and more as a continuously evolving system that adapts to new technology and contributions from other users. The long-term viability of an open system depends on the community that maintains it. We intend to establish a clear contribution model that lowers the barrier to adding features, fixing bugs, and extending documentation, together with a versioning scheme that lets users track changes and reproduce earlier configurations reliably. Adding features also include implementation of new modalities or data streams. Since MMLab is built on the LabStreamingLayer architecture, any LSL-compatible device can in principle be integrated. Additions such as eye-tracking, electromagnetic articulography (EMA), Electroencephalography (EEG) and others would broaden the range of questions the MMLab can address, particularly those linking overt communicative behavior to underlying physiological states.

    Alongside new developments, future efforts shall also focus on lowering the technical barrier. Containerization (e.g. using Docker) could package the software environment so that setup no longer depends on manual installation and dependency management; configuration templates could provide validated starting points for common recording scenarios; a lightweight graphical interface would make stream selection and session control accessible without the command line; and a quality-control dashboard would surface inter-stream offsets, dropped frames, and other diagnostics for inspection immediately after each session. Together, these would move the MMLab closer to usability by researchers without a strong programming background.

    Taken together, these developments point toward what we see as the central promise of the MMLab: not simply a cheaper alternative to commercial systems, but a broadening of who can do multimodal research, where, and on whom. Because the setup is portable, it enables recording in contexts outside the lab – naturalistic and field settings, developmental populations such as caregiver-infant dyads, and older or clinical groups for whom travelling to a lab is itself a barrier to participation. Because it is affordable and open, it lowers the threshold for laboratories or individuals without the budget for proprietary systems, and for research communities and regions in which such equipment has simply been out of reach, widening both the range of questions that can be asked and the diversity of populations from which answers are drawn. And because every component, from acquisition to analysis, is documented and inspectable, studies built on the MMLab are reproducible by construction, and the setup itself becomes a shared, extensible foundation that the community can adapt rather than a black box that each lab must trust on faith. Multimodal behavior is among the richest windows onto human behavior. Our hope is that the MMLab helps open that window to a wider range of researchers, settings, and participants than current tools allow.
