Introduction

Human behavior is multimodal and dynamic in nature. From the precise integration of visual, somatosensory and vestibular information when walking (MacKinnon 2018), to the effortless coordination of speech, gestures and facial expressions when communicating (Holler and Levinson 2019), our actions continuously unfold through multiple sensory, haptic and motor signals that interact across time and space. Yet, researchers investigating different aspects of human behavior in the context of psychological and neurocognitive sciences have traditionally relied on temporally coarse-grained, unimodal measures: e.g., capturing only one type of signal at a time, often through indirect proxies such as self-report questionnaires (Baumeister, Vohs, and Funder 2007), button presses, or accuracy scores (Maselli et al. 2023; Parsons, Kruijt, and Fox 2019).

In recent years, however, the research landscape in cognitive science has changed dramatically. Technological and methodological advancements in sensing equipment, recording devices and computational tools that handle high-dimensional data have paved the way for a new era of behavioral research, moving several interdisciplinary fields from unimodal to multimodal approaches Yang (2019). In the last decade alone, more than sixty thousand scientific publications within social sciences have appeared with the term “multimodal” in their keywords, marking a 400% increase from the previous decade (based on a Scopus keyword search conducted in 2025).

In contrast to the past, where measurements were limited to either unimodal proxies or laborious manual labelling of observed behaviors (e.g., Brugman and Russel 2004), nowadays researchers can employ high-frequency sensing devices to simultaneously capture movements, speech, physiological changes, gaze patterns, and even neural activity, as they unfold in real time. For instance, optical motion tracking systems such as OptiTrack (Nagymáté and Kiss 2018) can be combined with wearable eye trackers (Gunawardena, Ginige, and Javadi 2022) and electrophysiological recordings (Moon and Lee 2023) to study complex intra- and inter-personal brain-behavior dynamics of phenomena such as neural synchrony (e.g., Koul et al. 2023), cognitive workload (e.g., Charles and Nixon 2019) or psychosocial stress (e.g., Richer et al. 2024). Accordingly, the progress in technological infrastructures has enabled a similar progress in the level of spatio-temporal granularity with which researchers can ask and answer questions about different neuro-cognitive-behavioral phenomena (Laudańska et al. 2025).

Despite these technological advancements, there are important methodological challenges that will shape the future directions of this emerging field. To start, building a functioning multimodal laboratory from scratch can be both technically complex and prohibitively costly for many researchers. Firstly, one needs to select the appropriate recording system for each modality, a choice that can be particularly overwhelming given the sheer number of different manufacturers, pricing ranges and technical specifications. For instance, there are currently dozens of commercially available motion tracking (MT) systems, spanning a wide range of technologies – from high-precision optical systems (e.g., Vicon, Qualisys, Optitrack) to wearable inertial sensors (e.g., Xsen, Noraxon), to markerless computer vision tools (e.g., OpenPose, MediaPipe) – and costing from few hundred, to hundreds of thousands euros (for a comprehensive review, see Juveriyah, Himanshu, and Moharkan 2023).

Secondly, assembling all multiple recording devices (MT, microphones, physiological sensors, etc.) in a spatially efficient and modular setup without interfering with participants’ natural behaviors presents design and logistical challenges. For instance, many commercially available setups are only tailored to fixed laboratory environments, rely on heavy infrastructure, and are primarily designed for single-participant recordings, making them difficult to relocate or adapt the laboratory to real-world settings and/or multiple participants (Ladouce et al. 2017; Maselli et al. 2023).

Thirdly, a major challenge lies in integrating and synchronizing the heterogeneous data streams that all of these recording devices produce. In fact, each modality (whether MT, audio, gaze or physiology) is sampled at different rates (e.g., ranging from 60 Hz for videos to 16 kHz for audio), recorded using different hardware and software, and encoded in different formats (Vieira and Cunha 2026). Ensuring that all these signals are temporally aligned with sufficient precision and stored in an integrated and accessible format is non-trivial, especially in studies involving many recording devices in highly naturalistic paradigms with multiple interacting participants.

Finally, even after data collection, researchers are faced with the substantial demands of managing and analyzing multi-dimensional, time-varying datasets (such as dozens of 2D video streams, high-resolution audio, and continuous physiological signals) all of which need to be pre-processed, and organized for analysis. Without standardized pipelines or centralized data structures, this process can become fragmented and error-prone, especially when integrating data from multiple participants or sessions.

In most cases, researchers have outsourced these challenges to proprietary companies that manufacture recording systems with accompanied blackboxed software, with further help from technical support staff in the case of high-resourced institutions. To capture participants movements, one might indeed conveniently opt for a state-of-the-art MT system from a proprietary company that usually offers extensive customer support (including installation), optimal recording accuracy, and a user-friendly software with built-in analyses packages (Merriaux et al. 2017; van der Kruk and Reijne 2018). While these proprietary solutions offer clear advantages, they also come with some important drawbacks: they are often prohibitively expensive (e.g., Qualisys: circa 150-200K €, Vicon: 200-250K €), relying on closed-sourced software and analyses toolkits, often with monthly subscription costs (van Schaik and Dominici 2020). As a result, they are not accessible to many researchers or institutions working with limited budgets (e.g., in low-middle income countries), nor easily adaptable for use in non-standard experimental setups or in field-based research. Additionally, even when the budget allows, the issue of data integration and synchronization across different devices (e.g., an MT system with physiological sensors) still remains, as each proprietary device (typically) operates within closed device/software ecosystems that can make cross-platform integration challenging at many levels.

Given this context, a central question within the field of multimodal research is: how much should we rely on these proprietary systems, and how much should we instead invest in building intellectual capital toward more affordable, open-source alternatives? Beyond the drawbacks outlined above, an exclusive reliance on proprietary commercial systems can also hinder the sharing of knowledge required to build and operate multimodal setups, which risks being locked behind licenses, paywalled documentation, or the institutional memory of well-funded labs. Therefore, we believe that investing in alternatives can help democratize access to multimodal research and foster a more equitable and collaborative scientific ecosystem, where researchers engage in open pedagogical practices and actively contribute to the development, adaptation, and dissemination of methodological tools (Woelfle, Olliaro, and Todd 2011).

With this in mind, here we introduce the Mobile Multimodal Laboratory (MMLab). The MMLab project provides an affordable, open-source solution to anyone interested in capturing multimodal human behaviors in both controlled and ecological environments. It comprises a suite of freely available resources – i.e., the present paper, a detailed Quarto manual and a comprehensive coding repository – specifically designed to guide users through all the steps entailed in building a fully functional mobile multimodal lab, including synchronizing, recording, and analysing data streams of interest. The MMLab was built around the following key features:

  1. Accessibility: all documentation, code, and tutorials are open sourced to ensure broad accessibility, transparency, and reuse across research communities.
  2. Affordability: the proposed setup (video recording optimized for 3D motion tracking, audio-recording, and physiological signals from two participants) can be fully assembled for under 10,000€, as it relies on low-cost components and, whenever possible, non-proprietary hardware and software.
  3. Portability: MMLab is designed to be compact and easily transportable, allowing researchers to use it in a range of testing environments beyond traditional lab spaces.
  4. Built-in Integration and Synchronization: data from each recording device is centrally integrated through the LabStreamingLayer (LSL, Kothe et al. 2025), which allows for precise temporal synchronization of multimodal streams at the level of recording.
  5. Modularity: the setup supports a wide range of interchangeable components and sensors compatible with LSL, allowing researchers to customize the lab to suit specific research goals.
  6. Community-maintenance: MMLab aims to gather researchers interested in expanding and maintaining the current infrastructure to ensure longevity of the project, solve common troubleshooting issues, or implement demands from the community.
